The idea in 60 seconds

"You shall know a word by the company it keeps" (J. R. Firth, 1957). Words that show up in similar contexts tend to mean similar things. word2vec turns that into numbers: every word gets a vector, and the vectors are trained so that a word's vector is good at predicting the words around it.

The paper proposes two very simple models. Skip-gram (shown here) takes one word and tries to guess each neighbour within a small window. CBOW does the reverse: it guesses the middle word from its neighbours. There is no non-linear hidden layer, just a lookup of the word's vector and a dot product with every candidate. That simplicity is the point: it is cheap enough to train on billions of words.

Something surprising falls out. Because "king" and "queen" share the royal contexts (throne, crown, palace) but differ in exactly the way "man" and "woman" differ (he vs she), the vectors end up with a consistent "gender direction" and a consistent "royalty direction". So king − man + woman lands next to queen. Relationships become directions you can add and subtract.

The paper measured this on 19,544 analogy questions (semantic ones like capital–country, and syntactic ones like big : biggest), and showed the simple models beat earlier neural language models while training in a fraction of the time.

Try this

  • Before training, try king − man + woman: the answer is random, because the vectors are random. Press Train and watch the answer settle on queen.
  • Watch the 2D map while it trains: the words start as a random jumble, then arrange themselves so that the dashed arrow (man → king) and the solid one (woman → ?) become parallel and the solid one ends on queen.
  • Try paris − france + italy, then make up your own: prince − boy + girl, tokyo − japan + spain, woman − man + boy.
  • Ask something the corpus never supports, like paris − france + king. The best match has a much lower similarity: there's no consistent direction to follow.

How this demo simplifies the paper

  • The corpus is about 1,500 short sentences generated from templates in this page, with an 89-word vocabulary. It was built on purpose so that gender, royalty, age and capital-of relationships each have their own consistent contexts. The paper trained on a 6-billion-word Google News corpus with a 1-million-word vocabulary, where these regularities have to emerge from messy real text.
  • Vectors have 10 dimensions instead of the paper's hundreds (up to 1,000), and the plot is a PCA projection down to 2D of just the words in your question's group (the 8 people, or the 10 places). The analogy search uses the full 10D vectors, as in the paper (cosine similarity, excluding the three input words).
  • We compute a full softmax over all 89 words for each prediction. The paper used hierarchical softmax (a Huffman tree) so cost grows with log(vocabulary) instead. With 89 words the full softmax is cheap and gives the same kind of vectors. Negative sampling, the other popular trick, came in a follow-up paper.
  • Plain SGD with a learning rate decaying linearly to near zero, a fixed window of 2 words, no subsampling of frequent words. Training runs live in your browser and takes a few seconds.
  • Only skip-gram is shown. The paper's CBOW model and its comparisons with older neural language models are not reproduced.
