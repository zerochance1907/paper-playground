The idea in 60 seconds

You would expect a deeper network to be at least as good as a shallower one: the extra layers could simply learn to pass their input through unchanged. In practice, the paper found the opposite. Stacking more plain layers made the training error go up, not just the test error. That rules out overfitting. The deep network is simply harder to optimise. The paper calls this the degradation problem.

The fix is a tiny change in wiring. Every pair of layers computes a correction F(x) and adds it back to its own input, so the block outputs F(x) + x. That added x is the skip connection (or shortcut). It costs no extra parameters.

Now "do nothing" is the easy default: if F's weights are near zero, the block is an identity. Each block only has to learn a small residual on top of what earlier layers already did. The shortcuts also give the error signal a direct path back to the early layers during training, so every layer keeps getting useful updates.

With this change, the paper trained networks of 152 layers (and over 1,000 in an experiment) where deeper finally meant better. ResNets won ImageNet 2015, and the same x + F(x) pattern is now in nearly every deep model, Transformers included.

Try this

  • Press Train at 30 hidden layers. The green residual curve drops and its spiral fills in, while the red plain net barely moves off 0.69 (a coin flip).
  • Compare red with the grey 4-layer net: the deep plain net does worse than a much shallower one, even on its own training data. That gap is the degradation problem.
  • Slide hidden layers down to 4 and train again: plain and residual now learn about equally well. Skip connections matter most when the net is deep.
  • Watch the gradient bars. In the deep plain net, the bars near the input are tiny or empty (zero), so those layers can't learn. The residual net keeps them alive.

How this demo simplifies the paper

  • The networks are tiny fully connected nets (12 units per layer) classifying 300 points on a 2D two-spiral task. The paper used convolutional nets of 18 to 152 layers on ImageNet and CIFAR-10 images.
  • No batch normalisation. The paper used batch norm after every convolution and argued that its plain nets did not have vanishing gradients, yet still trained badly. In this toy, without batch norm, the deep plain net's gradients really do shrink or die (its ReLUs switch off). The symptom is the same, higher training loss with more depth, but the mechanism here is simpler than the one the paper describes.
  • To keep the residual net stable without batch norm, the second layer of each residual block starts with weights 10× smaller than normal, and all three nets clip the gradient norm to 1. These are common modern tricks, not part of the original paper.
  • Training is plain SGD with momentum on minibatches of 32 for a few thousand steps, live in your browser. The curves show this toy's loss, not the paper's results. Different random inits (try New random init) give somewhat different curves, and occasionally a residual run gets stuck too.
  • Each residual block has two layers and an identity shortcut, as in the paper's basic block. We skip projection shortcuts, downsampling and the bottleneck design.
