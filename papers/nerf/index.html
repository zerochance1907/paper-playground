3 · Learn the field from the training cameras

The idea in 60 seconds

NeRF stores a scene as a field: a function you can ask "at this point in space, how dense is the stuff here, and what colour is it?". In the paper the field is a neural network taking a 3D position and viewing direction. There are no meshes and no voxels of surfaces, just density σ and colour everywhere.

To make a picture, shoot one ray per pixel and take samples along it. Each sample's density gives an opacity α = 1 − e−σδ for the short segment δ. The transmittance T says how much light makes it that far without being blocked. The pixel is the sum of colours weighted by T·α. Solid things stop the ray almost at once; fog lets some light through. This is classic volume rendering, and every step is smooth.

Because it's smooth, you can run it backwards. Render the training cameras' pixels, compare them with the real photos, and follow the gradient of the error to adjust the field's density and colour everywhere along each ray. Rays from many directions must agree, so the only consistent answer is the actual 3D shape. Then you can render it from new viewpoints the cameras never saw.

Here the world is flat (2D) and each "photo" is a single row of pixels, but the rendering equation and the training loop are the same ones the paper uses.

Try this

  • Tap the top strip on the orange disc. In panel 2 the blue T line drops off a cliff and nearly all the weight lands on a few samples at the surface. Now pick a pixel whose ray crosses the green fog first: T dips only a little, so the pixel mixes fog green with what lies behind it.
  • Press Train and switch to Learned field: blurry blobs sharpen into the disc and square, and the bottom strip starts matching the top one from your camera.
  • After training, look inside the learned disc: it is often hollow. No camera ray ever gets past the surface, so the photos say nothing about the inside, and the field never learns it.
  • Set Training cameras to 2 and train again. It matches both photos, yet from other angles it is full of streaks and ghosts. With only two views, many different scenes explain the photos equally well.

How this demo simplifies the paper

  • The world is 2D and each camera image is one row of 64 pixels. The paper works in 3D with real photos of hundreds of thousands of pixels each.
  • The learned field is a 32×32 grid of numbers with bilinear interpolation, trained with Adam. The paper uses an 8-layer MLP with positional encoding of the inputs, a key ingredient for fine detail that this demo leaves out. The rendering and the gradient-through-rendering are the same idea.
  • Colour does not depend on viewing direction here, so there are no specular highlights. The paper's field also takes the ray direction as input.
  • One set of evenly spaced, jittered samples per ray. The paper also uses hierarchical "coarse-to-fine" sampling, which puts extra samples where the coarse pass found matter.
  • The "photos" are rendered from a hand-made scene of soft shapes with a black background, so the training data is noise-free.
