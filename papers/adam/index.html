The idea in 60 seconds

Training a network means walking downhill on a loss surface with millions of directions. Plain gradient descent (SGD) steps by learning rate × gradient. That's a problem when some directions are steep and others nearly flat: a learning rate small enough for the steep direction makes progress in the flat one painfully slow.

Adam keeps two running averages for every parameter separately: the average gradient m (like momentum, it smooths out noise) and the average squared gradient v (how big the gradients usually are). Each step is α · m / √v. Dividing by √v cancels out the scale: a parameter with tiny, consistent gradients takes the same-sized steps as one with huge gradients. Steps are roughly α at most, whatever the gradients look like.

That makes Adam invariant to rescaling the gradient: multiply the loss by 100 and SGD blows up; multiply by 0.01 and SGD freezes. Adam's path doesn't change. When gradients are noisy, m shrinks relative to √v, so Adam automatically takes smaller, more careful steps near the bottom.

Both averages start at zero, so early on they are biased towards zero. The paper fixes this with bias correction: divide m by (1 − β₁ᵗ) and v by (1 − β₂ᵗ). This idea, on top of RMSProp's √v scaling and momentum's averaging, is a big part of why Adam works with its default settings on so many problems.

Try this

  • On the Ravine, watch SGD drop to the valley floor and then crawl sideways, while Adam heads more directly to the minimum. The learning-rate panel shows why: SGD gives x and y the same rate, Adam gives the flat x direction a much bigger one.
  • Slide Gradient scale to ×100: SGD and momentum diverge. Slide to ×0.01: they barely move. Adam traces the same path every time, because its per-parameter rates shrink or grow by exactly the opposite factor.
  • Switch to the Saddle. Starting almost exactly on the ridge, SGD hardly feels the slope sideways and stalls. Adam rescales the tiny gradient and rolls off.
  • Turn off bias correction. While v is still close to its zero starting value, Adam's early steps come out several times too big, and its path overshoots. Switch it back on to see the paper's fix.

How this demo simplifies the paper

  • The "model" has two parameters, and the loss surfaces are simple formulas chosen by hand. The paper's experiments train logistic regression, multilayer nets and convolutional nets on MNIST, IMDB reviews and CIFAR-10.
  • Adam uses the paper's defaults β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.999, ε = 10⁻⁸, but a learning rate α = 0.05 instead of 0.001 so that paths are visible within a few hundred steps. Each competitor uses one fixed learning rate on every surface, chosen by hand. A tuned SGD could do better on any single surface, but its best learning rate changes when the gradient scale changes. Adam's mostly doesn't, which is the point.
  • The ravine is aligned with the axes. Adam scales each parameter separately, so it helps most when steep and flat directions line up with individual parameters. On a diagonal ravine its advantage is smaller.
  • "Noisy bowl" adds Gaussian noise to every gradient to imitate minibatch noise. Real minibatch noise has more structure.
  • The paper also proposes AdaMax and analyses convergence with regret bounds. Those parts are not shown here.
