The idea in 60 seconds

A normal neural network (an MLP) puts a learnable number on every connection and a fixed, hand-picked nonlinearity (like tanh or ReLU) on every node. A Kolmogorov-Arnold Network flips this: every connection carries a learnable one-dimensional function, and nodes only add up what arrives. Each function is a smooth B-spline curve defined by a handful of control points, and those control points are what training adjusts.

The design comes from the Kolmogorov-Arnold representation theorem: any continuous function of several variables can be written as sums of one-variable functions, f(x, y) = Φ(φ1(x) + φ2(y)) and so on. The theorem's functions can be badly behaved, but the paper argues that for the smooth, structured functions we meet in science, small KANs with smooth splines work well.

The payoff is interpretability. Because each edge is a curve you can plot, you can just look at a trained KAN and read off what it computed. Fit exp(sin(πx) + y²) and the edges turn into a sine, a parabola and an exponential. The paper takes this further by snapping edges to symbolic formulas; the "≈" labels here do a simple version of that. An MLP's weights, by contrast, are just a bag of numbers.

The paper also reports that KANs can reach lower error than MLPs with the same number of parameters on such functions. The error chart lets you see that on a toy scale.

Try this

  • Let it train on exp(sin(πx) + y²). Within a few seconds the x-edge becomes a sine wave and the y-edge a parabola; after 10–20 seconds the top edge settles into an exponential, and the labels say so.
  • Drag a control point on the selected edge while paused: the KAN heat map changes only along the matching direction, because that edge only sees one input. Press Train and watch it get pulled back.
  • Compare the error curves: on exp(sin(πx) + y²) and sin(πx) + y² the KAN typically ends 5–20× below the parameter-matched MLP within half a minute.
  • Switch to x · y. A product isn't a single sum of one-variable functions, so the KAN needs two hidden nodes. The paper shows a KAN can learn it as squares, x·y = ((x+y)² − (x−y)²)/4, but this small, briefly trained one usually finds a less readable solution and only ties the MLP. KANs are not automatically interpretable or better.

How this demo simplifies the paper

  • The KAN shape is fixed per target ([2, 1, 1] or [2, 2, 1]) and matches the target's known structure. The paper also shows how to discover the shape by training a bigger KAN with sparsity regularisation and pruning it.
  • Each edge is a pure cubic B-spline (5 grid intervals on the first layer, 8 on the second). The paper adds a residual SiLU term with its own weights to every edge, and refines the grid during training ("grid extension"); neither is done here. Inputs outside a spline's range are clamped to the edge of the range.
  • Edges start as straight lines with random slopes (the paper starts splines near zero plus a SiLU term). Both networks are trained with Adam on 256 random points; the paper mostly uses LBFGS.
  • The "≈ sin / x² / exp" labels fit a·f(b·u + c) + d for a few candidate functions and pick the best R². The paper's symbolic step is similar in spirit but interactive and also fixes the affine parameters exactly.
  • The MLP baseline is a single hidden layer sized to roughly match the KAN's parameter count. A wider or deeper MLP would also fit these functions; the paper's accuracy claims concern scaling at matched size and are debated for larger, noisier tasks.
