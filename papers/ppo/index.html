The idea in 60 seconds

In reinforcement learning, data is expensive: the agent has to act in the world to get it. A policy-gradient method collects a batch of experience with the current policy, then nudges the policy to make good actions (positive advantage A, better than average) more likely and bad ones less likely. Ideally you would squeeze many gradient steps out of each batch.

The catch: the batch only tells you about actions near the old policy. The standard surrogate objective r·A, where r = πnew(action) / πold(action) is the probability ratio, keeps rewarding you for pushing r further. Take enough steps and the policy bets everything on whatever looked best in a small, noisy batch. Those "destructively large" updates are why vanilla policy gradient takes one step per batch.

PPO's fix is one line: maximise min(r·A, clip(r, 1−ε, 1+ε)·A). Once a good action's ratio passes 1+ε, or a bad action's falls below 1−ε, that sample's objective goes flat and stops contributing gradient. Nothing more is gained by moving further, so you can safely run many epochs on the same batch. Taking the min makes it a pessimistic bound: clipping only ever removes the incentive to move, never adds one.

That gives most of the stability of the earlier trust-region method TRPO, with plain first-order gradient steps and a few lines of code, which is why PPO became the default RL algorithm for everything from games and robots to fine-tuning language models.

Try this

  • Press +10 steps a few times. The red policy keeps piling probability onto one arm and its KL keeps climbing; the blue one stops once its ratios reach about 1±ε, and its KL levels off.
  • Watch panel 2: as blue ratios cross the clip line, the numbered dots turn hollow. Those samples no longer push the policy.
  • Slide ε from 0.05 to 0.5: the plateau moves, and so does how far PPO lets the policy move per batch.
  • Hit Collect new batch repeatedly (with 10+ steps each time). PPO keeps improving steadily. The unclipped run can lock onto a mediocre arm, then only ever pulls that arm and never learns better.

How this demo simplifies the paper

  • The environment is a 5-armed bandit (one step, no states) with hand-set reward means and Gaussian noise, not a MuJoCo or Atari task. The policy is just 5 logits, not a neural network.
  • The advantage is the reward minus the batch mean, divided by the batch standard deviation. The paper estimates advantages over multi-step episodes with a learned value function and generalized advantage estimation (GAE).
  • Each "gradient step" is one plain full-batch gradient ascent step. The paper uses Adam on minibatches for K epochs, and adds a value-function loss and an entropy bonus to the objective.
  • Both runs see exactly the same samples (same random numbers), so the only difference between red and blue is the clipping. The red "unclipped" curve is the paper's no-clipping baseline in spirit; its benchmark numbers are not reproduced here.
  • Clipping does not strictly cap the ratio. A ratio can still drift past 1±ε through the shared softmax, as you can sometimes see here. That is true of real PPO too.
