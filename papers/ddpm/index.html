The idea in 60 seconds

Destroying a picture is easy: keep adding a little random noise until nothing is left. The forward process does exactly that in T small steps, and it has a handy closed form: the noisy version at step t is just x_t = √ᾱ_t · x_0 + √(1−ᾱ_t) · ε, a shrunken copy of the data plus Gaussian noise ε. Scrub the slider and you are evaluating that formula.

A diffusion model learns to run this film backwards. The paper's key simplification is what the network should predict: given a noisy point xt and the step t, guess the noise ε that was added. Training is just "noise some data to a random step, ask the network for ε, penalise the squared error". That is the whole loss (the paper's Lsimple), and it is what this page is doing live, thousands of times a second.

To generate, start from pure noise at t = T and repeatedly subtract a bit of the predicted noise, add back a little fresh randomness, and step to t − 1. Each step only has to remove a small amount of noise, which is an easy job, yet a hundred easy jobs in a row turn a shapeless blob into new points that look like your data.

The arrows on the right show what the network has learned: at every location and timestep it points "toward the data". At large t they all point roughly to the middle; as t shrinks they start to point at the fine structure of your shape.

Try this

  • Slide t from 0 to 100 on the left: the shape blurs, shrinks toward the centre and becomes a plain Gaussian blob. That endpoint is where generation starts.
  • Press Generate right after Reset network, then again after 20 or so seconds of training. Early samples are a fuzzy cloud; later ones trace the shape.
  • After generating, scrub t on its own: the right panel replays the reverse trajectory, and you can see that the coarse layout appears first (large t) and the fine detail last (small t).
  • Pick blank, draw your initials, and wait for the loss to settle before generating.

How this demo simplifies the paper

  • The data are 2D points instead of 32×32 or 256×256 images, and the denoiser is a small MLP (2 inputs + 16 time features → 64 → 64 → 64 → 2, SiLU) instead of the paper's 35.7M-parameter U-Net with self-attention.
  • T = 100 steps instead of the paper's 1000. The paper's linear β schedule (10−4 to 0.02) is stretched by 10× (10−3 to 0.2) so the data is still fully destroyed by t = T.
  • For the three preset shapes the page starts from weights that were pre-trained offline for 25,000 steps by exactly the training code on this page (it takes about two minutes, too long to make you wait), and then keeps training live. Reset network and the blank canvas start from random weights, so you can watch training from scratch; expect blurry samples for the first 20–30 seconds. The paper trains for hundreds of thousands of steps on large image datasets.
  • Besides x and y, the network also gets sin/cos features of the coordinates. This is a standard trick that helps a small MLP learn sharp 2D shapes quickly; the paper's U-Net works on pixels and does not need it.
  • The objective (predict ε, unweighted squared error), the sinusoidal timestep embedding, the closed-form forward process and the sampling rule (the paper's Algorithm 2 with σt2 = βt) are the paper's, used as written.
  • Each data point keeps one fixed noise draw in the left panel so scrubbing looks smooth. During training, fresh noise is drawn every time.
