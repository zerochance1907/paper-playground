The idea in 60 seconds

A GAN learns to produce fake data that looks real by pitting two networks against each other. The generator G takes random noise z and turns it into a sample x = G(z). The discriminator D looks at a sample and outputs the probability that it came from the real data rather than from G. Nobody ever tells G what a "good" sample is: its only teacher is D.

Training alternates. D takes a few steps to get better at separating real from fake (the blue curve rises over the grey bumps and falls over the orange ones). Then G takes a step in the direction that makes D more wrong: it moves its samples uphill on D's curve, which is toward wherever real data sits and fake data doesn't. The thin lines at the bottom show that G is just a function from z to x; training bends that function.

The paper's key result: for a fixed G, the best possible discriminator is D*(x) = pdata(x) / (pdata(x) + pg(x)) (the dashed line). Against that D*, G's objective equals the Jensen-Shannon divergence between the real and generated distributions (plus a constant), so the game's optimum is exactly pg = pdata, where D* = ½ everywhere and the discriminator can only guess.

The paper also notes a practical catch. Early on, D easily rejects G's samples, so log(1 − D(G(z))) is flat and G gets almost no gradient. Training G to maximize log D(G(z)) instead has the same fixed point but much stronger gradients. And if G is trained too much without updating D, it can collapse many z values onto one output, the problem later called mode collapse.

Try this

  • Pick two bumps and let it train. Watch D's curve flatten toward ½ and the JS divergence fall as orange settles onto grey.
  • Drag one grey peak far to the side mid-training. D instantly spikes over the uncovered region, and G's samples are pulled after it.
  • Set D steps per G step to "G×3" (the generator outruns the discriminator) on three bumps. Look for orange piling onto one or two peaks and hopping between them instead of covering all three.
  • Switch the generator loss to the original minimize log(1 − D(G(z))) and restart: progress at the start is much slower, because D confidently rejects G and the gradient vanishes.

How this demo simplifies the paper

  • Data is one-dimensional (a mixture of Gaussians you control) instead of images. The paper's Figure 1 uses the same 1D picture to explain the idea; its experiments use MNIST, TFD and CIFAR-10.
  • Both networks are tiny: G is 1→16→16→1 and D is 1→32→32→1 (tanh hidden units), trained with Adam at batch size 64. The paper used maxout/ReLU networks, dropout in D and SGD with momentum; Adam did not exist yet.
  • The noise z is a single uniform number in [−1, 1]. Because G is a continuous function of one number, it can only cover separated bumps by stretching thinly across the gap between them, which you can see as orange "bridges". Real GANs use higher-dimensional noise.
  • The ideal D* and the JS divergence are computed from histograms over 80 bins, which is only possible because the data is 1D and its true density is known. Real GANs never get to see these numbers.
  • The convergence proof in the paper assumes D is trained to optimality and G is updated in function space. With small networks and finite steps, as here, training can oscillate or stall; that is a real property of GANs, not a bug in the demo.
