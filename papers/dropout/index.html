The idea in 60 seconds

A big neural network trained on a small dataset can memorise it: it bends its decision boundary around every noisy point, gets the training data perfectly right and then does worse on new data. That is overfitting.

Dropout's fix is simple. On every training step, each hidden unit is switched off at random with probability p (the paper uses 0.5). The network has to make its prediction with whichever random half of its units survived. On the next step, a different half survives.

Because any unit might vanish, no unit can rely on a specific partner to fix up its mistakes. The paper calls this preventing co-adaptation. Each unit has to learn something that is useful on its own, and those features tend to be simpler and more robust.

At test time nothing is dropped. Instead every unit's outgoing weights are scaled by (1 − p), so the expected input to the next layer matches training. The paper shows this cheaply approximates averaging the predictions of a huge ensemble of "thinned" networks, all sharing weights, one for each pattern of dropped units.

Try this

  • Press Train. Some training points have the wrong colour on purpose. The no-dropout net reaches 100% training accuracy by bending its boundary into little islands around them. The dropout net mostly ignores them.
  • Watch the loss chart: the red dashed training loss dives to zero, while the red solid test loss turns around and climbs. That gap is overfitting. Green's gap stays much smaller.
  • Turn on Show test points to see 500 new points the nets never trained on, and compare the test accuracy under each map.
  • Drag the drop rate to 0.8 and retrain: too much dropout and the net underfits. Try 0.1: barely different from no dropout.

How this demo simplifies the paper

  • The task is 60 2D points from two interleaved half-moons, with 15% of the labels flipped at random (so even a perfect model scores about 85% on test points), and a net with two hidden layers of 64 ReLU units. The paper tested on MNIST, TIMIT speech, CIFAR-10, ImageNet and Reuters text with much larger networks.
  • Only hidden units are dropped. The paper also dropped 20% of the input pixels, which makes little sense with only two input coordinates.
  • The paper combined dropout with a "max-norm" limit on each unit's incoming weights and a decaying learning rate. We use plain full-batch SGD with momentum and nothing else, so you see dropout's effect alone. Because of that, if you let training run long enough, the dropout net slowly starts to overfit too.
  • Both networks start from the same random weights and train live in your browser. The exact numbers change with every new dataset; the pattern (a smaller train/test gap with dropout) is what to look for, not the size of the gain.
  • The unit grid shows the dropout pattern for one training example. In the real algorithm, and in this demo's training, every example in every step gets its own random pattern.
