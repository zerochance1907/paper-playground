The idea in 60 seconds

Before this paper, language models mostly read a sentence one word at a time, passing a memory along like a game of telephone. Attention lets every word look directly at every other word in one step, so long-range connections are easy and the whole sentence can be processed in parallel.

Each word plays three roles. As a query it asks "what am I looking for?", as a key it advertises "here's what I am", and as a value it carries the information to hand over. The match between a query and a key is their dot product. Softmax turns the scores into weights that add up to 1, and the word's new representation is the weighted average of all the values.

The paper's small but crucial trick is dividing the scores by √d. In d dimensions, dot products of random vectors grow like √d, so without scaling the softmax saturates into a near one-hot choice and its gradients vanish. Panel 2 shows exactly this.

When generating text, a word must not peek at words that come after it. The decoder applies a causal mask, setting those scores to −∞, so their weights become zero. The full Transformer stacks this operation many times, with several "heads" in parallel, each learning its own way to form queries, keys and values.

Try this

  • Drag mat right next to cat, then tap cat: most of its attention moves to mat.
  • Drag a word far from the centre. Longer vectors produce bigger dot products, so the weights get sharper.
  • Turn on Causal mask: the upper-right triangle of the matrix goes to zero, because no word can attend to a later word.
  • In panel 2, slide d up to 1024: the unscaled softmax collapses onto a single key, while the scaled one stays informative.

How this demo simplifies the paper

  • Word vectors are 2D and placed by hand (you move them); the paper learns 512-dimensional embeddings.
  • Queries, keys and values are the word vectors themselves. The paper first multiplies them by learned matrices WQ, WK, WV.
  • One attention head instead of eight, and no positional encodings, residual connections, layer norm or feed-forward layers.
  • Panel 2 uses random Gaussian vectors to show the scaling effect. It is the paper's argument (section 3.2.1), not a measurement from a trained model.
